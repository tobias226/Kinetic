# KINETIC — a guide without the jargon

This guide explains the app in everyday language. **All opportunities, investors, contacts, introductions, amounts and example ratings are fictional**. The product is an independent interview demonstration, not an official Copley Point system.

## The three areas

**1. Systems Thinking → Find Opportunities:** An investment opportunity could be a real-asset business or group of assets that might benefit from funding and operational expertise. The app helps show what to research, where a potential opportunity sits in the process and what action might come next.

**2. Relationship Networks → Build Partnerships:** A capital partner is an investor who might fund an opportunity. Choosing the right one is about shared goals and practical ability to work together — not just the largest amount of money. The app shows possible matches and what must be verified by a person.

**3. Second-Order Effects → Assess Value:** A project can seem attractive on day one but less attractive once running costs, emissions or other future consequences are taken into account. The app compares simple refurbishment and rebuilding scenarios.

## Three ideas behind KINETIC

**Systems thinking:** Assets, customers, energy infrastructure, regulations and investment capital are interconnected. An opportunity can only be understood properly by asking how those pieces affect one another. In KINETIC, this is why public market observations flow into a question and then into a next action.

**Relationship networks:** Selecting a suitable partner is more than searching for the largest cheque. It involves understanding preferences, building trust and finding a credible conversation route. The animated network and the sample relationship map illustrate this *concept*, not real people.

**Second-order effects:** A decision has consequences beyond its first immediate result. Rebuilding might lower annual energy use but require more capital and create upfront carbon emissions. Refurbishment might cost less initially but leave higher future running costs. Neither option is automatically preferable.

## What do the key words mean?

| Term | Simple explanation | What it **does not** mean |
|---|---|---|
| **First-look checklist score** | A 0–100 example score for whether an opportunity ticks criteria such as location, sector, asset backing and growth potential | A return forecast, proof of an investable asset or an internal Copley Point score |
| **Investor profile** | An invented example of the sectors and capital amounts an investor may prefer | Verified live appetite, genuine available capital or a real investment mandate |
| **Business fit** | Whether an investment seems consistent with a potential investor's sector, approximate funding amount, geography, period and structure | Proof that an investor wants the opportunity |
| **Ease of introduction** | Whether a relationship or trusted mutual contact could help begin a conversation | Permission to contact or evidence of approval |
| **Importance of an existing contact** | An optional slider that changes the balance between business fit and introductions in a demonstration score | The value or strength of a real relationship; it cannot outweigh actual due diligence |
| **Follow-up task** | A reminder to call, research, confirm information or seek an introduction | An instruction to invest |
| **Carbon from construction** | Emissions associated with carrying out the refurbishment or build, counted up front in this simplified model | A whole-building verified lifecycle assessment |
| **Annual operating emissions** | An estimate of the emissions each year after the building is used | Measured emissions or an exact future forecast |
| **Carbon payback** | In the right circumstances, how many years of lower annual emissions it takes to offset higher initial construction emissions | Investment payback, profit or net present value |
| **Second-order effects (looking beyond the first decision)** | Thinking about later costs, risks and consequences instead of only immediate cost | An advanced predictive model or a financial recommendation |

## What is scored in the investor tool?

The fictional **business-fit score** is out of 100 points: sector 35, approximate capital size 30, UK geography 10, approximate holding period 10 and willingness to consider different funding arrangements 15. The app separately calculates a fictional **ease-of-introduction score** from a sample contact strength and whether an introductory route is captured. By default, the combined illustrative score uses 80% business fit and 20% ease of introduction. Those weights are choices made for the demo, **not** verified investment criteria.

Many important matters are **left unscored deliberately**: actual capital available, risk and return requirements, leadership quality, governance, sustainability expectations, reputation, conflicts and how investment decisions are approved. These must be checked with evidence.

## What are the biggest limitations?

The app does not connect to live investors, real deals, real contact networks, a private CRM or a secure partner portal. Changes remain in the current Streamlit session unless exported. The refurbishment comparison uses invented inputs and simple undiscounted costs, not a complete asset valuation. Public research is dated and linked, but it must be rechecked if used later.

In an interview, lead with the commercial question: **“How could a small, relationship-led investment team use better information to choose its next conversation?”** The app helps illustrate a possible workflow; it is not claiming to know the correct commercial answers.
